Theoretical Background & Formulas

Collision Theory

Chemical reactions occur when reactant particles collide with one another. According to **Collision Theory**, a collision will only result in a chemical reaction (a "successful" collision) if:

  • Energy: The colliding particles possess combined kinetic energy greater than or equal to a minimum threshold, known as the **Activation Energy ($E_a$)**.
  • Orientation: The particles collide in a correct spatial orientation (simplified to energy threshold in this simulation).

Factors Affecting Reaction Rates

The rate of a chemical reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. It can be modified by:

  • Concentration (Quantity): Increasing the initial particle count leads to more crowded conditions, directly increasing the collision frequency.
  • Volume (Pressure): Compressing the chamber decreases the space particles occupy, forcing them closer together and raising the frequency of collisions.
  • Temperature: Raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles. According to the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, this increases:
    • The *speed* of particles (thus collision frequency).
    • The *fraction* of collisions that exceed the activation energy.
  • Catalysts: A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a **lower activation energy ($E_a$)**. It increases the reaction rate without being consumed.

Arrhenius Concept & Rate Law

The temperature dependence of the rate constant $k$ is given by the Arrhenius equation: $$k = A \cdot e^{-E_a / RT}$$ The rate of reaction for $A + B \rightarrow C$ is modeled qualitatively by:

Rate = k · [A] · [B] Where [A] and [B] are reactant concentrations. The rate decreases over time as A and B are consumed, which is represented by the leveling-off of the curves.
