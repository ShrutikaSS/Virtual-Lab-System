Theoretical Background & Formulas

The Concept of pH

The term pH stands for "potential of Hydrogen" (or power of Hydrogen). It is a logarithmic scale used to specify the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution. First introduced by Danish chemist Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen in 1909, it represents the concentration of hydronium ions ($H_3O^+$ or simply $H^+$) in solution:

pH = -log₁₀[H₃O⁺] Where [H₃O⁺] is the active concentration of hydronium ions in moles per liter (mol/L).

Self-Ionization of Water and Kw

Water molecules continuously react with one another in a reversible self-ionization (autoionization) process: 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. At equilibrium (at 25°C), the product of the concentrations of these ions is constant, known as the water ionization constant ($K_w$):

Kw = [H₃O⁺] · [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C)

Taking the negative logarithm of both sides of this equation yields a highly practical relationship that links acidity and alkalinity indicators:

pH + pOH = 14 Where pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻] represents the hydroxide ion concentration.

Classification of Solutions

Based on the balance of hydronium and hydroxide ions at 25°C:

  • Acidic Solutions: pH < 7. Hydronium ion concentration exceeds hydroxide ion concentration ($[H_3O^+] > [OH^-]$).
  • Neutral Solutions: pH = 7. Hydronium and hydroxide concentrations are exactly equal ($[H_3O^+] = [OH^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M}$).
  • Basic (Alkaline) Solutions: pH > 7. Hydroxide ion concentration exceeds hydronium concentration ($[OH^-] > [H_3O^+]$).

Common Substances Across the pH Scale

Below are representative pH values of familiar chemicals at 25°C:

  • pH 0.8: Battery Acid (highly corrosive, high $[H_3O^+]$)
  • pH 2.0 - 3.0: Gastric acid, lemon juice, vinegar
  • pH 5.0 - 6.5: Coffee, milk, saliva
  • pH 7.0: Pure distilled water (perfectly balanced)
  • pH 8.0 - 9.0: Sea water, baking soda solution
  • pH 11.5 - 12.6: Household ammonia, bleach
  • pH 13.9: Drain cleaner (highly alkaline, high $[OH^-]$)
